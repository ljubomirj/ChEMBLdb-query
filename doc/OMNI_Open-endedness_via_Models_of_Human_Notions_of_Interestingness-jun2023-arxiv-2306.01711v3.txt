Here’s the paper **“OMNI: Open-endedness via Models of human Notions of Interestingness”** explained step by step in plain language. ([arXiv][1])

## 1) What problem are they trying to solve?

Open-ended learning is the dream where an agent **keeps learning new skills forever**. The headache is: there are *tons* of possible tasks.

Even if you filter to tasks the agent can currently learn (using “learning progress”), you still get lots of **junk tasks**:

* tiny variations,
* repetitive busywork,
* “learnable but boring” stuff.

So the missing piece is: **how do we prioritize tasks that are not only learnable, but also *interesting* (worthwhile/novel)?** ([arXiv][1])

## 2) Their key idea (the OMNI punchline)

They propose OMNI: use a **foundation model** (think large language model / multimodal model) as a proxy for **human notions of “interestingness.”**

Why might that work?

* FMs are trained on huge amounts of human text/images.
* Humans mostly write about what they find *interesting*, meaningful, non-trivial.
  So the FM implicitly learns patterns of “this seems noteworthy vs mundane.”

They call this an FM-based **Model of Interestingness (MoI)**. ([arXiv][1])

## 3) What OMNI actually does (mechanically)

OMNI isn’t replacing learning-progress curricula; it **adds a “boringness filter” on top**.

### Step A — Start with a standard learning-progress (LP) curriculum

The agent estimates where it’s improving fastest and samples those tasks more. ([ar5iv][2])

### Step B — Ask the FM: “is this task boring?”

The FM/MoI predicts whether candidate tasks are “interesting” or “boring” (based on how humans would likely see them). ([ar5iv][2])

### Step C — Downweight boring tasks hard

If a task is labeled boring, OMNI multiplies its sampling weight by something tiny (they mention **0.001**), so it *almost never* gets trained on. ([ar5iv][2])

**Result:** the agent mostly trains on tasks that are **(1) learnable now, and (2) interesting**.

## 4) Where do the tasks come from?

They test in two styles of settings:

### A) Finite task sets (benchmark environments)

They use environments like **Crafter** and **BabyAI**, where you can enumerate a big set of tasks and track progress across them. ([ar5iv][2])

### B) “Infinite” / growing task space

They also show a more open-ended setup where tasks are **generated during training** using a pretrained FM (they describe using GPT-4 to propose next tasks and even specify success-check “state conditions”). ([ar5iv][2])

This is meant to mimic true open-endedness: you can’t pre-list every task, so you **grow the task set** as the agent improves.

## 5) What experiments show (high level)

Across the benchmarks, they compare:

1. **Uniform sampling** (pick tasks randomly)
2. **Learning progress only**
3. **OMNI** (learning progress + FM interestingness)

They report OMNI:

* learns **more tasks**,
* gets **higher overall success**,
* even though it *biases away* from many tasks (because those were deemed boring). ([ar5iv][2])

In the infinite/growing-task setting, they evaluate only on tasks that were ever sampled (since you can’t evaluate infinite tasks), and OMNI completes **more tasks above a success threshold** than baselines. ([ar5iv][2])

## 6) Why this is conceptually important

This paper is basically saying:

> “Learning progress tells you what’s learnable next.
> Foundation models tell you what’s *worth learning* next.”

It reframes FMs as **task-picking engines** (auto-curriculum designers), not just text generators. ([arXiv][1])

## 7) Limitations / things to be skeptical about

A few natural “watch-outs” the paper itself motivates:

* **Whose interestingness?** The FM reflects the training data’s human preferences/biases.
* **Shortcut risk:** “Interesting” might correlate with surface novelty rather than deep skill-building.
* **Domain dependence:** works nicely in these benchmarks; the harder question is how robust it is in messier real-world task spaces.

(Those are the big practical concerns you’d want in mind when reusing the idea.)

## Section 1 — Introduction

### What they’re worried about

The long-term goal in RL is **open-ended learning**:

> an agent that never stops discovering new skills.

But in practice, two big problems show up:

1. **Too many tasks**

   * The task space is huge (or infinite).
   * Most tasks are irrelevant, redundant, or trivial.

2. **Learning-progress alone isn’t enough**

   * Popular solution: train on tasks where performance is improving fastest.
   * Problem: this still includes a lot of *boring* tasks.

     * Tiny variations
     * Busywork
     * Skills that don’t unlock anything new

So you get agents that are **busy but not meaningfully expanding their abilities**.

### The key missing ingredient

Humans don’t just ask:

> “What can I learn next?”

We also ask:

> “What is *worth* learning next?”

That second notion — *interestingness* — is missing from most curricula.

### Their big idea

Foundation models (LLMs, VLMs) are trained on **human-produced content**.

Humans mostly produce:

* surprising things
* useful things
* meaningful things
* non-trivial things

So FMs implicitly learn a statistical notion of:

> “Humans find this interesting.”

**OMNI = use an FM as a proxy for human interestingness to guide task selection.**

## Section 3 — Method: OMNI

This is the core. Let’s go slowly.

---

### 3.1 The overall loop (big picture)

At every training step:

1. Propose candidate tasks
2. Estimate **learning progress** on each task
3. Estimate **interestingness** using a foundation model
4. Combine the two
5. Sample tasks to train on

Think of it as:

> **LP tells you *what you can learn***
> **FM tells you *what’s worth learning***

---

### 3.2 Learning Progress (LP) recap

They don’t reinvent LP — they reuse standard ideas.

Roughly:

* Track success rate over time for each task
* Compute improvement (slope or delta)
* Prefer tasks with **positive recent improvement**

This ensures:

* No wasting time on tasks already mastered
* No wasting time on tasks too hard (no progress yet)

LP answers:
**“Is this task learnable *right now*?”**

---

### 3.3 Model of Interestingness (MoI)

This is the novel part.

#### What is the MoI?

A foundation model classifier that predicts:

* Is this task *boring* or *interesting* (from a human perspective)?

They don’t require the FM to be perfect.
They just need it to **roughly agree with human intuition**.

#### How does the FM see a task?

Tasks are represented in natural language or symbolic descriptions like:

* instructions
* goal descriptions
* environment states
* task names

The FM outputs a score or label.

#### Important detail

They don’t *rank everything finely*.

They mostly do:

* **binary-ish filtering**
* boring → almost zero probability
* interesting → normal LP-based probability

This makes the system robust to noisy FM judgments.

---

### 3.4 Combining LP and Interestingness

This is intentionally simple.

If:

* LP(task) = learning progress score
* MoI(task) = {interesting, boring}

Then sampling weight is roughly:

```
if boring:
    weight = LP * 0.001
else:
    weight = LP
```

So:

* Boring tasks are not forbidden
* They’re just **almost never sampled**

This is crucial:

> OMNI is *soft biasing*, not hard constraints.

---

### 3.5 Finite vs Infinite Task Sets

They handle two scenarios:

#### Finite tasks

* Tasks are predefined
* OMNI filters among them

#### Infinite / growing tasks

* Tasks are generated over time (using an FM)
* OMNI decides which generated tasks are worth pursuing

This is where open-endedness really comes in.

## 1️⃣ OMNI as pseudocode (clean + intuitive)

Think of OMNI as a *task-sampling wrapper* around any RL system.

```python
# Maintain stats
for task in task_set:
    success_history[task] = []
    interestingness[task] = FM_judge(task)  # {interesting, boring}

while training:
    for task in task_set:
        # Estimate learning progress
        LP[task] = slope(success_history[task][-k:])

        # Apply interestingness filter
        if interestingness[task] == "boring":
            weight[task] = LP[task] * 0.001
        else:
            weight[task] = LP[task]

    # Normalize sampling distribution
    P = normalize(weight)

    # Sample a task
    task = sample(P)

    # Train agent
    result = train_on(task)

    # Update stats
    success_history[task].append(result)
```

### Key intuition

* **LP** keeps learning *efficient*
* **FM filter** keeps learning *meaningful*
* Nothing is hard-forbidden → system stays flexible

If you remove the FM line, you get a standard LP curriculum.

## 2️⃣ Relation to curiosity & intrinsic motivation

This is where the paper quietly reframes a whole subfield.

---

### Classical intrinsic motivation (simplified)

Most intrinsic rewards look like:

[
r_{int}(t) = | \hat{s}*{t+1} - s*{t+1} |
]

or

[
r_{LP}(t) = \left| \text{error}_{t-k} - \text{error}_t \right|
]

Interpretation:

* “I’m curious because I’m surprised”
* or “I’m curious because I’m improving”

**Problem:**
Surprise ≠ importance

A TV with static noise is infinitely surprising and totally useless.

---

### What OMNI adds (conceptually)

OMNI introduces a **prior over task value**:

[
P(\text{task is worth learning})
]

estimated from **human data**, not agent error.

So the effective objective becomes:

[
\text{Train on task} \propto
\underbrace{\text{Learnability}}*{\text{LP}}
\times
\underbrace{\text{Worthwhileness}}*{\text{FM prior}}
]

This is *huge* conceptually:

> Curiosity asks: *“Can I learn something?”*
> OMNI asks: *“Is this the kind of thing that matters?”*

---

### Why this works better

Humans have already solved (imperfectly!) the value-selection problem:

* which skills compound
* which discoveries unlock others
* which variations are redundant

Foundation models compress that judgment.

OMNI is basically:

> **Bayesian curiosity with a human-derived prior**

## 1) An OMNI-style loop for your ADMET + MD pipeline

### Step-by-step plan

1. **Define what a “task” is** in your setting
   Pick *one* of these (you can extend later):

   * **Task = pick a molecule** (to label / simulate / curate)
   * **Task = pick an endpoint** (LogD, solubility, permeability, ER, etc.)
   * **Task = pick a “region” of chemical space** (scaffold cluster, property quantile bucket, etc.)
   * **Task = pick a simulation action** (run MD replicate, longer trajectory, alternative excipient condition)

2. **Define “learning progress” (LP)**
   LP should be “how much better did the model get *recently* on this task bucket?”

   * For molecule buckets: Δ(validation error on that bucket over last N training rounds)
   * For endpoints: Δ(endpoint-specific MAE/RMSE)
   * For clusters/scaffolds: Δ(cluster error or Δcalibration

3. **Define “interestingness” (MoI) for chemistry**
   The crucial trick: MoI isn’t “fun”; it’s **scientific/value relevance**. Examples:

   * “Mechanistically informative” (explains why solubility changes with excipient)
   * “Delivery-relevant” (connects to absorption/permeability/lymphatic uptake)
   * “Novel but plausible” (not just weird outliers / assay noise)

4. **Combine them like OMNI**

   * Sample probability ∝ **LP × InterestingnessWeight**
   * If “boring”: multiply by 1e-3 (or 1e-2 if you want gentler filtering)

### A concrete implementation that fits your stack

**Task bucket definition (good default):**

* Bucket = (Butina scaffold cluster) × (endpoint) × (label-density tier)
  where label-density tier = {dense, medium, sparse}

**Learning progress:**

* Keep a rolling window of per-bucket validation MAE
* LP(bucket) = MAE(t−k) − MAE(t)  (positive = improving)

**Interestingness model options (pick one to start):**

* **Heuristic MoI (fast & robust)**

  * Interesting if bucket is:

    * sparse labels (information gain potential)
    * high uncertainty but not extreme outlier
    * known delivery-critical endpoints (e.g., solubility/permeability/ER)
    * under-represented scaffolds
* **LLM-assisted MoI (closer to the paper)**

  * Feed bucket summary text to an LLM:

    * scaffold family name / nearest known drugs
    * endpoint context (“inhaled dissolution”, “oral excipient solubilization”, etc.)
    * uncertainty + predicted error + any mechanistic descriptors
  * Ask for: “interesting vs boring” + one-line justification
  * Cache results; you don’t want to call it repeatedly.

**Pseudo-loop**

* Every training “epoch block” (say every 1–3 hours of training):

  1. recompute LP across buckets
  2. update uncertainty estimates (ensemble / MC dropout / conformal residuals)
  3. compute MoI label for buckets (cached)
  4. sample next batch of *data acquisition actions*:

     * which molecules to curate next
     * which MD sims to run next
     * which assay endpoints to target next

**Why this is powerful**
It turns your pipeline into a **closed-loop system**:

> the model decides what data to acquire next, but under a value filter that keeps it from chasing trivial wins.

## 2) Formalising “interestingness” as a latent variable

Here’s a clean, ML-friendly formulation that matches the OMNI spirit but is more “scientific ML” than “prompting.”

### Step-by-step formal model

1. **Introduce a latent variable**

* Let ( z \in {0,1} ) denote “interesting” (1) vs “boring” (0)
* Each *candidate task* ( \tau ) (bucket/molecule/experiment) has features ( x_\tau )

2. **Model interestingness probabilistically**

* ( p(z=1 \mid x_\tau) = \sigma(g_\phi(x_\tau)) )
* ( g_\phi ) can be:

  * a small classifier
  * a graph model + metadata
  * an LLM-score distilled into a smaller model

3. **Define learnability / learning progress**

* Let LP((\tau)) be your measured recent improvement (as above)
* Or use expected improvement:

  * ( \mathbb{E}[\Delta \text{error} \mid \tau] )

4. **Task sampling distribution**
   A direct OMNI-like rule:

[
P(\tau) \propto \text{LP}(\tau),\big(\epsilon + p(z=1\mid x_\tau)\big)
]

* (\epsilon) is small (like 1e-3) so boring tasks aren’t impossible.

### How to train (p(z=1|x)) in practice (3 options)

**Option A — Distill from an LLM (closest to paper)**

* Prompt LLM to label tasks as interesting/boring
* Train (g_\phi) on those labels
* You now have a cheap MoI you can run at scale

**Option B — Learn from human/PI preferences**

* If you ever choose “these experiments matter,” log that
* Use those choices as supervision for (z)

**Option C — Self-supervised proxy**
Define “interesting” as tasks that:

* unlock downstream improvements broadly (transfer)
* increase coverage/diversity
* reduce uncertainty in high-impact regions
  Then (z) is learned as “predicts downstream value.”

### Why this latent-variable view is useful

* You can measure calibration: “Is my MoI overconfident?”
* You can debug: “What features are driving boringness?”
* You can combine multiple notions (mechanistic + novelty + feasibility) as separate latents.

## 3) Is this proto-AGI, or just solid engineering?

Both, but in different senses. Here’s a clear way to think about it.

### Step-by-step framing

1. **AGI-ish ingredient: value-guided exploration**
   Open-endedness needs two things:

* exploration (try new stuff)
* value (try *the right* new stuff)

OMNI injects a **learned value prior** (“humans tend to care about X”).

That’s a real ingredient of generally intelligent behaviour:

> choosing problems that compound.

2. **But it’s not “full AGI”**
   Why not?

* It doesn’t create a grounded, self-consistent objective system
* It borrows human preferences via a model trained on human artifacts
* The “interestingness” signal can be shallow, biased, or misaligned

3. **It’s best understood as “curriculum alignment”**
   OMNI aligns the agent’s self-generated curriculum with:

* human salience
* human usefulness priors
* human compression of what tends to matter

That’s powerful, but still *a tool*, not autonomy.

### The practical verdict

* **Engineering win:** yes — it’s a clean way to reduce wasted learning.
* **Scientific win:** yes — it gives you a framework for “what’s worth testing next.”
* **AGI relevance:** yes — but as a *component* (value selection), not a whole agent.

### The failure mode to watch (important)

If your MoI is wrong, you can get:

* **premature narrowing** (miss weird-but-important regions)
* **trend-following** (learn what literature talks about, not what’s true)
  So you want the “epsilon leak” (boring tasks occasionally sampled), plus periodic exploration.
